1 Introduction [cite: 10]

[cite_start]

The speed-accuracy tradeoff in cognitive tasks is essential for understanding decision-making under time pressure[cite: 11]. Previous research by Katsimpokis et al. (2020) [cite_start]found that linear collapsing boundaries were a better fit for deadline conditions compared to fixed boundaries[cite: 12, 14]. [cite_start]These researchers used Evidence Accumulation Models to simulate the brain's decision-making process[cite: 13]. [cite_start]There is a potential for non-linear collapsing boundaries to more realistically capture urgency effects[cite: 15]. This project report discusses the effect of using an exponentially collapsing decision boundary to explain decision-making, based on empirical data collected by Katsimpokis et al. (2020) [cite_start][cite: 16]. [cite_start]The report investigates whether an exponentially collapsing boundary improves the fit to Speed-Accuracy-Tradeoff model data compared to linear or fixed models[cite: 17]. [cite_start]We used Bayesflow to simulate trials and to understand the challenges in recovering parameters like drift rate, initial boundary threshold, and exponential decay rate[cite: 18].

[cite_start]

2 Data [cite: 19]

[cite_start]

The dataset for this project was obtained from the OSF repository, which was linked in the Katsimpokis et al., 2020 paper[cite: 20]. [cite_start]The original study involved three experiments on speed-accuracy tradeoffs (SAT)[cite: 20]. [cite_start]Experiments 1 and 2 each had 24 participants, with a different set of 24 participants in Experiment 3[cite: 21]. [cite_start]The participants, aged 22-23 years (60% female), were given monetary compensation or research credits[cite: 21]. [cite_start]All experiments included a training phase to familiarize participants with the task, followed by a test phase where SAT manipulations were applied and data was collected[cite: 22].

[cite_start]

Experiment 1, called the Random Dot Motion task, required participants to determine the direction of motion of dots on a screen[cite: 23]. [cite_start]Experiment 2, the Flash task, had participants decide which of two circles flashed faster[cite: 24]. [cite_start]The test phases for both Experiment 1 and 2 used a 2x2 within-subjects design[cite: 25]. [cite_start]The first factor was whether participants were cued to respond fast or accurately, and the second was whether a response deadline was present or not[cite: 26, 27]. [cite_start]This created four conditions: speed cue with deadline, speed cue without deadline, accuracy cue with deadline, and accuracy cue without deadline[cite: 28]. [cite_start]The order of these four conditions was counterbalanced across participants[cite: 29]. [cite_start]Each of the four conditions consisted of 200 trials, for a total of 800 trials in the test phase[cite: 31]. [cite_start]Experiment 3 was included to address the possibility that Experiment 2 was too fast for some participants, and it included early and late deadlines[cite: 32]. [cite_start]Consequently, the design was a 2x3 within-subjects design with two levels for cue-based SAT and three for deadline-based SAT (early, late, and no deadline)[cite: 33].

3 Statistical Model [cite: 39]

[cite_start]

Since the trials in the dataset do not have an explicit time-series component, each trial can be treated as an independent observation[cite: 40]. [cite_start]Our goal is to estimate decision-making parameters under different cue and deadline conditions[cite: 41]. [cite_start]This allows us to consider each trial, regardless of the participant, as an independent data point[cite: 42].

[cite_start]

3.1 The Drift Diffusion Model [cite: 43]

[cite_start]

We used the Drift Diffusion Model (DDM) as the foundational evidence accumulation framework[cite: 44]. [cite_start]The DDM posits that decisions are the result of a noisy process where evidence accumulates over time until it reaches a decision threshold, or boundary[cite: 45]. [cite_start]As seen in Figure 1, this process involves the continuous accumulation of evidence with noise[cite: 46]. Mathematically, the change in accumulated evidence over a small time-step $dt$ is given by:

$$dx=\nu\cdot dt+\sqrt{dt}\cdot\mathcal{N}(0,1)$$
[cite_start]

Here, $ν$ is the drift rate, which represents the average rate of evidence accumulation, and the noise term $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ captures random fluctuations[cite: 50, 51]. [cite_start]A decision is made when the accumulated evidence reaches either the upper boundary (e.g., choice A) or the lower boundary (e.g., choice B)[cite: 52]. [cite_start]Higher drift rates result in faster and more accurate decisions, while lower drift rates lead to slower and less accurate ones[cite: 53]. [cite_start]For this model, we used a simplified DDM without inter-trial variability in the drift rate or starting point[cite: 54]. [cite_start]We restricted our estimation to three parameters: [cite: 55]

    [cite_start]
  • • $v$: Drift rate (evidence quality) [cite: 56]
    • [cite_start]
  • • $k$: Exponential decay rate of the boundary over time [cite: 57]
    • [cite_start]
  • • $a_{0}$: Initial boundary height [cite: 58]
[cite_start]

3.2 Exponential Boundary Collapse [cite: 62]

[cite_start]

While the standard DDM uses fixed decision boundaries, the effective boundary height may decrease over time under time pressure, a phenomenon known as collapsing boundaries[cite: 63]. [cite_start]Previous research has used a linear collapse model[cite: 64]. [cite_start]However, human decision urgency often increases non-linearly under deadlines[cite: 64]. [cite_start]We implemented an exponential boundary collapse function defined as: [cite: 65, 67]

$$a(t)=\alpha_{0}\cdot(1-e^{k\cdot(t_{max}-t)})$$
[cite_start]

where: [cite: 66]

    [cite_start]
  • • $\alpha_{0}$ is the initial boundary height at $t=0$ [cite: 69]
    • [cite_start]
  • • $k$ is the decay constant that controls the rate of collapse [cite: 70]
    • [cite_start]
  • • $t$ is the elapsed decision time [cite: 71]
    • [cite_start]
  • • $t_{max}$ is the upper limit for the decision time [cite: 72]
[cite_start]

This function allows for a slower initial decline in boundary height, followed by a sharper drop as time goes on, which may be a better reflection of how decisions are made under deadlines[cite: 73].

[cite_start]The base Gaussian and Gamma distributions were modified with cutoffs and spread conditions by multiplying and adding scalar values[cite: 76].

4 Approximator [cite: 79]

[cite_start]

The inference framework was implemented using the BayesFlow library, which separates the parameter inference process into two neural components: a summary network and an inference network[cite: 80]. [cite_start]We used a DeepSet architecture for the summary network, which is well-suited for handling trial-level data in an order-invariant manner, ensuring the network is not affected by the order of trials within a condition[cite: 81]. [cite_start]The DeepSet network had 8 layers and a Gaussian base distribution[cite: 104]. [cite_start]A dropout rate of 0.1 was set to prevent overfitting[cite: 104].

[cite_start]

The inference network was a FlowMatching normalizing flow model, which transforms the base Gaussian distribution in the parameter space into the learned posterior distribution over the parameters[cite: 105]. [cite_start]This flow component consisted of 8 coupling layers[cite: 106]. [cite_start]Normalizing flows were chosen because they can approximate flexible, non-Gaussian posterior distributions, which is important for drift-diffusion models where parameter posteriors can be skewed[cite: 106]. [cite_start]The initial learning rate was set to 0.001, and all inputs ($v, k, a_{0}$) and the response time output were standardized using the default method[cite: 107].

5 Training [cite: 108]

[cite_start]

The training dataset was composed of 5000 samples, with each sample containing 200 trials[cite: 109]. [cite_start]The model was trained for 50 epochs on this pre-generated dataset using a batch size of 100[cite: 110]. [cite_start]An offline training approach was used because simulating the exponential-boundary DDM is computationally expensive[cite: 111]. [cite_start]The datasets were generated once and then stored, allowing for experimentation with different network architectures without having to perform repeated simulations[cite: 112]. [cite_start]BayesFlow uses the Adam optimizer by default, which was the optimizer used for this project[cite: 113].

[cite_start]

6 Diagnostics [cite: 114]

[cite_start]

The training process showed a consistent decrease in loss, which is shown in Figure 4, indicating that the inference network converged stably[cite: 115]. [cite_start]However, a closer look at the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) plots in Figure 6 reveals that the drift rate parameter, $v$, deviates slightly from the 95% confidence interval, which suggests mild overfitting[cite: 116]. [cite_start]The ground truth vs. estimate plots in Figure 5 show that while $v$ was successfully recovered, the exponential decay rate ($k$) and the initial boundary height ($a_{0}$) could not be estimated reliably[cite: 120]. [cite_start]The posterior z-score contraction plots in Figure 3 indicate a systematic bias for $v$, but it is still within acceptable limits[cite: 121]. [cite_start]In contrast, $k$ and $a_{0}$ showed little to no learnable structure[cite: 122]. [cite_start]Exploratory data analysis in Figure 2 supports these findings, showing a clear relationship between $v$ and response times, but no such dependency is evident for $k$[cite: 123]. [cite_start]This implies that while the model is sensitive to the drift rate, it has difficulty capturing the dynamics of collapsing boundaries introduced by $k$ and $a_{0}$[cite: 123].

7 Inference [cite: 124]

[cite_start]

The posterior distributions (Figure 7) show consistent trends across all experimental conditions[cite: 125]. [cite_start]The estimated $v$ is highest in the speed cue with deadline condition, followed by the accuracy cue with deadline, and is lowest in the no-deadline conditions[cite: 126]. This is consistent with the behavioral results reported by Katsimpokis et al. (2020)[cite_start], which validates that the drift rate reliably captures task-induced urgency effects[cite: 127]. [cite_start]In contrast, the posterior estimates for $k$ and $a_{0}$ did not change across conditions, suggesting that the inference network was unable to distinguish between the effects of high and low values of these parameters, possibly because a relationship does not exist[cite: 128]. [cite_start]This limits the explanatory power of the exponential boundary collapse in its current implementation[cite: 129]. [cite_start]Posterior predictive checks (Figure 8) indicate that the model captures the response time distributions well, but struggles to correctly reproduce response accuracy[cite: 130].

8 Limitations & Potential Improvements [cite: 131]

[cite_start]

Limitations [cite: 132]

    [cite_start]
  • • **Weak signal in boundary parameters**: While the drift rate ($v$) could be recovered, both the exponential decay rate ($k$) and the initial boundary height ($a_{0}$) showed poor identifiability[cite: 133]. [cite_start]Simulations confirm that their effects only appear late in the trial, which makes them difficult to differentiate from noise[cite: 134].
    • [cite_start]
  • • **Collusion of $k$ and $a_{0}$**: The parameterization of the collapsing boundary creates trade-offs between $k$ and $a_{0}$, which limits their interpretability[cite: 135].
    • [cite_start]
  • • **Priors not fully informed by expertise**: The priors for $k$ and $a_{0}$ were chosen heuristically[cite: 136]. [cite_start]Without strong expert or empirical constraints, inference for these parameters is less stable[cite: 137].
[cite_start]

Potential Improvements [cite: 138]

    [cite_start]
  • • **Hierarchical inference**: Extending SBI to a hierarchical framework across subjects and conditions could pool information more efficiently, which would improve the recovery of weakly identified parameters[cite: 139, 518].
    • [cite_start]
  • • **Alternative boundary formulations**: Incorporating urgency gating or sigmoid-shaped collapse functions could produce more realistic urgency effects compared to the exponential form[cite: 519].
[cite_start]

9 Conclusion [cite: 520]

[cite_start]

This project demonstrated how Simulation-Based Inference (SBI) can be applied to study decision-making models that use collapsing boundaries[cite: 521]. [cite_start]The method successfully recovered the drift rate ($v$), which aligned with behavioral manipulations across speed-accuracy tradeoff conditions[cite: 522]. [cite_start]This confirms SBI's ability to capture dominant cognitive signals[cite: 522]. [cite_start]However, the recovery of the exponential boundary parameters ($k, a_{0}$) was found to be unreliable[cite: 523]. [cite_start]Simulation results confirmed that their behavioral signatures are weak and often confounded, which limits their explanatory power[cite: 524]. [cite_start]This highlights a key challenge in SBI: while it is powerful in settings where likelihoods are intractable, the inference depends critically on parameter identifiability and prior specification[cite: 525]. [cite_start]In summary, while SBI shows great promise for cognitive modeling, the dynamics of collapsing boundaries remain difficult to recover[cite: 526]. [cite_start]Future research should explore hierarchical inference, alternative collapse formulations, and stronger prior integration to improve recovery beyond the drift rate[cite: 527].
